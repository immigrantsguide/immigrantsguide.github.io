USCIS & U.S. Immigration History

A brief historical overview of the federal immigration service and the development of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS).

From Federal Immigration Administration to USCIS

1891 — Federal immigration administration begins

Congress established a federal role in administering immigration laws, replacing earlier state-level immigration inspection with federal administration.

1892 — Ellis Island opens

Ellis Island became a major federal immigration inspection station in New York Harbor and operated as an immigration station until 1954.

1903–1920s — Immigration laws expand

Federal immigration laws expanded over time, adding new grounds for exclusion and establishing national-origins quota systems in the 1920s.

1933 — INS is created

The Immigration and Naturalization Service (INS) was created by consolidating federal immigration functions.

1952 — Immigration and Nationality Act

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 consolidated and revised many federal immigration and nationality provisions.

1965 — Major immigration law changes

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 eliminated the national-origins quota system and established a preference system emphasizing family relationships and employment skills.

1986 — Immigration Reform and Control Act

IRCA created an employment verification system and legalization programs for certain people who met statutory requirements.

1990 — Immigration Act

The Immigration Act of 1990 revised employment-based and family-based immigration provisions and increased immigration levels in several categories.

2002–2003 — DHS and USCIS

The Homeland Security Act of 2002 reorganized federal immigration functions. On March 1, 2003, former INS functions were separated among USCIS, ICE, and CBP. USCIS assumed responsibility for immigration service functions such as adjudicating applications and petitions and naturalization.

Today — USCIS

USCIS is the DHS component responsible for administering immigration and naturalization benefits, including petitions, applications, and naturalization.

Important Note

This page is a general historical overview and is not legal advice. Immigration laws, regulations, policies, forms, and procedures can change.
